FINDING 01

Framing vocabulary diverges sharply. NYT uses science, policy, and alarm language at roughly 2–6× the density of Fox. Fox uses denial language at 2× NYT's rate — but both corpora discuss the phenomenon constantly.

FINDING 02

NYT has a starker headline-vs-body gap. "Climate crisis" appears in NYT headlines at 8.25 per 1,000 words but only 0.70 in body text — a 11.8× gap. Fox's equivalent ratio for "hoax" is 5.8×. Each outlet amplifies its preferred term in headlines more than it sustains it in reporting.

FINDING 03

Terminology has shifted across both outlets. "Climate crisis" (the more urgent framing) rose sharply in both corpora post-2021. "Hoax" peaked in Fox during 2017–2020 alongside Trump's first term and resurged in 2025–2026. "Global warming" declines as a primary term in both, replaced by "climate change."

FINDING 04

Fox's corpus is wider and more reactive. Fox articles span a greater date range and respond to political events (debates, hurricanes, EPA actions) while NYT articles cluster around policy and scientific findings. Fox articles are longer on average and more likely to quote opponents of climate consensus, even when reporting it.

Interpretation

"Climate change" is the dominant, neutral bureaucratic term in both corpora. "Global warming" has declined since 2014 in both, suggesting a coordinated (or organic) vocabulary shift. "Climate crisis" is the emergent escalation term — NYT adopts it in headlines; Fox quotes it sarcastically. "Hoax" is nearly absent from NYT body text but appears at meaningful density in Fox, almost always attributed to Trump or Republican figures.

Heuristic Reading

NYT's framing is scientistic and technocratic: science and policy language dominate, with alarm vocabulary present but calibrated. Fox's framing is politically reactive: science language drops sharply (suggesting less original reporting and more op-ed / punditry), while denial vocabulary doubles relative to NYT. Both corpora show alarm language at similar absolute levels — Fox frequently quotes or reports on others' alarm-language claims in order to contest them.

Headline vs. Body: Diagnostic Gap

Which corpus has a starker divergence between what the headline promises and what the body delivers? Higher gap = term appears more in headlines than the body text sustains.

Key Finding

NYT's biggest gap term is "climate crisis" — used 11.8× more densely in headlines than in body text. This suggests escalatory headline writing on a term the body uses more sparingly. Fox's biggest gap term is "hoax" (5.8×) and "con job" (19×), suggesting headlines lean into skeptical framing that articles dilute with quote-heavy reporting. Both outlets thus employ editorial amplification in headlines — but toward opposite rhetorical ends.

ERA FINDING

In the Fox corpus: "hoax" appears at elevated rates during 2017–2020 (Trump first term) then drops in 2021–2024 (Biden era) before resurging post-2025. "Climate crisis" increases 2.4× from era one to era two even in Fox, suggesting the term's cultural penetration was unavoidable — though Fox typically frames it in quotation marks or attributes it to opponents. "Global warming" declines across both eras in both outlets.

Pattern Summary

The concordance reveals a fundamental editorial asymmetry: NYT uses climate terms as subject — climate change causes, threatens, requires action. Fox uses climate terms as object of contestation — who said it, who disputes it, whether it is being used as a political tool. This is visible even when both outlets use identical vocabulary: "climate change" in NYT means the phenomenon; in Fox it often means the claim.
